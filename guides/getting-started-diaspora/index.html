Getting started, if the family left

If your grandparents or their parents emigrated, the best records for the first step are often not in Minsk or Vilnius. They are the papers created after the family arrived, which sometimes still contain the old place — spelled however the clerk heard it.

Write down only what someone actually knows

On one page, for the person whose origins you care about:

  • The name they used in the new country, and any other spelling the family insists on.
  • Who they came with, or who they sent for later. Siblings are often the better clue.
  • A year of birth and a year of emigration, even if both are approximate. Mark them as approximate.
  • Religion, if anyone is sure. “They were Christian” is weaker than Catholic, Orthodox, or Jewish, and it is still worth recording as unsure.
  • The place-name as it was said at home, in whatever language and however it was spelled on an envelope. Do not “correct” it to a modern city yet.

Leave blank what nobody knows. A blank is more useful than a guess that later looks like a fact.

Read the papers you already have

Naturalization files, passenger lists, draft cards, marriage licences in the new country, and petitions for relatives often name a town or a province. Read the image, not only the index. Indexes drop diacritics and sometimes drop the place entirely.

One carefully read manifest is worth more than a week of unfocused searching in databases of the old country. Those databases cover a fraction of the books, in a fraction of the religions, for a fraction of the years. A miss there is not a miss in the archive.

If you have a DNA test, keep the list of matches. Do not treat a suggested “ancestral journey” as a place of birth. It is a regional hint, and it belongs in the “open” column until a document agrees. The page on how we research says what we will and will not do with it.

Decide which question you are asking

Two shapes cover almost every enquiry we can assess:

  • One line. The emigrant’s parents, and then their parents, in a single direction. This is the right brief when you can point at one person and say “I want to know where this person was from, and who the parents were.”
  • Broader research. More than one line, a couple who came from different places, or a story (“we were minor nobility”, “we left because of a particular estate”) that has to be tested before the right line is obvious.

You do not need to know the parish before you choose. You do need to know which question you want answered first. A brief that says “everything about the family” cannot be assessed; a brief that says “her parents, and the town on the manifest” can.

When it is reasonable to stop and ask

Ask for help when the place-name will not settle, when you do not know the religion, when the records you can see online simply stop, or when the language on the page is not one you read. Those are ordinary stopping points, not a sign that the family left no trace.

They are also not a sign that a researcher can promise a particular depth. Some lines move quickly once the parish is known. Some stop because the book is gone. The assessment is where we say which of those your papers suggest — and what a first stage would cost. The orientation most people hear back is somewhere from about €1,000 to €10,000 or more, depending on the question. That sentence is not a quote.
