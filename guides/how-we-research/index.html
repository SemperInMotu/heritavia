How we research

A family tree on a screen is a picture. The thing we are hired to build is a chain: each person tied to a parent by evidence you could show someone else. This page is that standard, not a tour of every source.

One link at a time

Research starts from a person who is already identified — usually someone in the papers you have — and walks back to a parent. Then it stops and asks the same question again. Jumping from a surname in a database to a medieval namesake, or to a famous bearer of the name, is how families acquire ancestors they are not related to.

A line is that chain in one direction: one parent, that parent’s parent, and so on. A wider investigation is several such chains, or a question that has to be settled before the right chain can even be chosen (which of two Janes emigrated; which religion the household used). The enquiry form asks you to say which of those you mean. The method underneath is the same.

Three kinds of sentence

In the report, a statement is one of these. They are not mixed to make a smoother story.

  • Record. A document says it, and the citation is enough to open that document again. “The register of this parish records a marriage on this date” is a record. The register’s silence is also a record, if we looked.
  • Inference. More than one record points the same way, and we say so. Two men of the same name in the same village are not automatically one man. When we decide they are, the reasons are written down, so you can disagree with the step without losing the documents.
  • Open. We do not know. A family story stays a family story until a record meets it. An open question is a result. Filling it with a plausible person is not.

Negative searches are logged. “Not in this book” stops the next person — you, or us, a year later — from paying to read it again under the impression that nobody tried.

What DNA is for

A DNA test you already have is evidence of a different kind. Shared matches can suggest a region, support a hypothesis, or retire one. They almost never name a parish, a parent, or a year. We do not sell tests, and we do not treat a match list as a tree.

When DNA and documents disagree, the disagreement stays visible. The tempting move — letting the test “confirm” a namesake in the wrong province — is the move the labels are there to prevent. The older how-to notes this site used to publish about particular matching websites were a manual. They are not the method. The method is the separation above.

What we will not imply

We will not tell you that a line reaches a given century, that a given number of ancestors will be found, or that the work ends in certainty. Some fonds are rich. Some burned. The report’s job is to show which of those you are looking at, for the question you actually asked.

The public Polesie file is an example of the format. It is not a depth we attach to the next commission.
